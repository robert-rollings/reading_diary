I have read a number of books on the transatlantic slave trade and have taken an interest in the American Civil War. No book has brought the horror and injustice of chattel slavery so viscerally to light before. Each stinking overseer and bite of the whip felt real. I was moved to tears while sitting on the bus, and was asked by my neighbour if I was alright. The complex interiority of James and the inferred turmoil of the boy Finn make this an exceptionally enjoyable read for me. The pace, emotional payoff, and connection with the characters is second to none. An important note that I would not have enjoyed this nearly so much if I hadn't read Twain's _The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn_ before. I tend to struggle with short stand-alone novels. They tend to lack the depth of character and immersion I need. That Everett achieved that in so few pages is astounding. But then again, is this really now a sequel? 

Everett states that James is in conversation with The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn and that was one of the most delightful aspects of the book for me. It felt a subversion of the wet abolitionism of Twain's work. That subversion depended on the nature of James' intellect but at the same time that intelligence provides the greater contrast with the Jim of Huckleberry Finn.

As I was reading I found myself empathising with James more and more. I asked myself why I felt for James in a way I did not feel for Jim in The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. The uncomfortable answer is that I saw James as an equal. In Huckleberry Jim is othered by Twain. He is simple, his language primitive and he is treated as Huck's faithful hound unto death. Twain directs the reader's sympathy onto Huck, it is only reflected back onto Jim. Here this is reversed, the same man, called Jim in one book and James in the other, and this time it is the man himself, not the boy beside him, that the reader is asked to see. We feel for James as a thinking and feeling human being. My moral discomfort came from realising that Jim was just as worthy of compassion as James. I had allowed myself to be duped by Twain. And in the end realised that code-switching is still a burden on my brothers and sisters of colour. A human is a human no matter their intellectual capacity or race.

Here Everett uses this device as a two edged sword. I imagine to readers of colour they relate directly to James and his fellow slaves as they act out their farce for the white man. Whereas white readers fall into the same trap I did and come to recognise it. A deft and masterly use of a plot device. Everett has, I believe, one simple requirement of his white readers. Empathy. He also demands justice as a necessary step towards healing. James refuses to be seen as a victim, and he knows that true justice must be between equals. Without justice there can be no healing.

> I chose the word enemy, and still do, as oppressor necessarily supposes a victim.

To be a victim you must be in another's power. James' refusal of victimhood gives him agency and dignity as a human. It is the white man that is wrong, he affirms that his state is not natural. He is a man demanding to be seen and respected as an adult amongst adults, a person amongst people. An equal.

The reflection on the modern day world and especially the United States is also clear. The historical injustice, crime against humanity and genocide of slavery and its continued impact on all of us is woven throughout the novel:

> "So I'm in a free state?" The men laughed. "Boy, you're in America."

The journey of Huck from racist to almost but not quite embracing his identity as James' son is profound. From seeing James as property he has stolen, "So, that's like stealin', right? If'n I took a mule from the side of the road, and I knowed who it belonged to, wouldn't that be stealin'?" to his father and only man to ever truly care for him creates a sense of unease: "Jim?" "Yes, Huck?" "So, I am a nigger?"

Like Noughts and Crosses this novel forces the white man to ask "what if", and in that the veil of ignorance can truly be brought down and empathy let in. I'm left with the question, will anyone ever be free from the prejudice and sins of the past. Everett knows that we are all slaves until we are all free.

> Maybe you won't be a slave, but you won't be free.

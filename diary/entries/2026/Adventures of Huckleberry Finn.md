I read _The Adventures of Hucklebery Fin_ in preparation for reading _James by Percival Everett_.

I really struggled through this novel. I saw Fin's voice as condescension by Twain. He at all times seemed a characterature of a southern country lad.

> "Yes," says I, and other times when things is dull, they fuss with the parlyment; and if everybody don't go just so he whacks their heads off. But mostly they hang around the harem".

Fin goes through trauma after trauma, the death of his mother, the abuse (and kidnap) of his father, and the horror of the feud between  the Grangerfords and the Shepherdsons. This makes Fin a mirror of Dickens' Oliver, or Coperfield. His psychological state is rarely anything but brave and forward looking. The onfly thing that makes me pause to consider Fin a product of a pen with no experience of the brutality of life is _Twain's_ own experience of horror and poverty in his youth.

This raises the question of cultural context. Horror was normal, parental death and cruelty more common. Perhaps reflecting a societal expectation that one just "get on with it".

Fin's morals sentiments improve and grow after time as he starts to see Jim as a human being. But nether the less a lower cast: he chooses to "go to hell" (for steeling) rather than betray Jim.

On to Jim. While it is clear that _Twain_ is an abolitionist. His portrayal of slavery and the ark of the plot prima facie show this. However the portrayal of Jim is very challenging for a modern reader. Jim is portrayed as a minstrel. The stereotype of his speech "I doan' mine one er two kings, but dat's enough" would be recognisable in a blackface cabaret act of the early 20th century.

Indeed Jim's character barely changes. He remains absolutely loyal (until death) of a white child. He is unable to have agency without the boy. He after all doesn't leave his island until Fin brings a canoe. Jim is passive where Fin is active.

Jim acts as father figure to Fin. His innate goodness is what changes Fin's attitude. But it is this very victorian sentimentality that makes me uncomfortable and reflects the difficulty people of color have today. To be taken seriously a person of color has to better than a white person. Even if Jim were stupid, lazy and selfish he is still worthy of compassion.

Though as a write this i'm reminded of Fin's father. Who I believe is reflected in Harper Lee's Bob Ewell. "Pap" is at the bottom of the barrel and his only consolation is that he is always better than a Black man. Is Twain making a direct contrast between Tom and Pap? Should one be disgusted at the difference in treatment between the two. One deserves jail and derision, the other deserves freedom and compassion?

However, the moral journey Fin goes on and his development from his own disdain to love for Tom is real. But I'm left wondering is it the love one has for a man or a loyal dog. I'm not sure Twain understood the difference when it comes to a black man.

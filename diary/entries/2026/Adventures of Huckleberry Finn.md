I read this in preparation for _[[James]]_ by [[Percival Everett]].

I struggled through the novel. Huck's voice read to me as condescension rather than characterisation, a caricature of a southern country lad rather than a person: _"Yes," says I, and other times when things is dull, they fuss with the parlyment; and if everybody don't go just so he whacks their heads off. But mostly they hang around the harem._

Huck goes through trauma after trauma: the death of his mother, the abuse and kidnap by his father, the horror of the feud between the Grangerfords and the Shepherdsons. This makes him a mirror of Dickens' Oliver, or Copperfield. Yet his psychological state is rarely anything but brave and forward-looking. The only thing that gives me pause before concluding that Huck is the product of a pen with no experience of the brutality of life, is Twain's own experience of horror and poverty in his youth. That raises a question of cultural context: horror was normal, parental death and cruelty more common, perhaps reflecting a societal expectation that one simply "gets on with it".

Huck's moral sentiments improve and grow over time as he starts to see Jim as a human being, though nonetheless a lower caste in his mind. He chooses to "go to hell" (for stealing Jim's freedom, in the eyes of the law) rather than betray him.

On to Jim. It's clear Twain is an abolitionist. His portrayal of slavery and the arc of the plot show this on their face. But the portrayal of Jim is very challenging for a modern reader. Jim is written as a minstrel figure: dialect like _"I doan' mine one er two kings, but dat's enough"_ would be recognisable from a blackface cabaret act of the early twentieth century.

Jim's character barely changes. He remains absolutely loyal, to the point of death, to a white child. He has no agency without the boy: he doesn't leave his island until Huck brings a canoe. Jim is passive where Huck is active.

Jim acts as a father figure to Huck. His innate goodness is what changes Huck's attitude. But it's this very Victorian sentimentality that makes me uncomfortable, and it reflects a difficulty people of colour still face today: to be taken seriously, a Black person has to be better than a white person. Even if Jim were stupid, lazy, and selfish, he would still be worthy of compassion.

Writing this, I'm reminded of Huck's father. I believe Pap is reflected in Harper Lee's Bob Ewell: at the bottom of the barrel, with his only consolation being that he's always better than a Black man. Is Twain making a direct contrast between Jim and Pap? Should one be disgusted at the difference in treatment between the two, when one deserves jail and derision and the other deserves freedom and compassion?

And yet the moral journey Huck goes on, his development from disdain to love for Jim, is real. But I'm left wondering whether it's the love one has for a person, or the love one has for a loyal dog. I'm not sure Twain understood the difference when it comes to a Black man.

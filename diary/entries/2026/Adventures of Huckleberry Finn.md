I read _The Adventures of Hucklebery Fin_ in preparation for reading _James by Percival Everett_.

I really struggled through this novel. I saw Fin's voice as condescension by Twain. He at all times seemed a characterature of a southern country lad.

> "Yes," says I, and other times when things is dull, they fuss with the parlyment; and if everybody don't go just so he whacks their heads off. But mostly they hang around the harem".

Fin goes through trauma after trauma, the death of his mother, the abuse (and kidnap) of his father, and the horror of the feud between  the Grangerfords and the Shepherdsons. This makes Fin a mirror of Dickens' Oliver, or Coperfield. His psychological state is rarely anything but brave and forward looking. The onfly thing that makes me pause to consider Fin a product of a pen with no experience of the brutality of life is _Twain's_ own experience of horror and poverty in his youth.

This raises the question of cultural context. Horror was normal, parental death and cruelty more common. Perhaps reflecting a societal expectation that one just "get on with it".

Fin's morals sentiments improve and grow after time as he starts to see Jim as a human being. But nether the less a lower cast: he chooses to "go to hell" (for steeling) rather than betray Jim.

On to Jim. While it is clear that _Twain_ is an abolitionist. His portrayal of slavery and the ark of the plot prima facie show this. However the portrayal of Jim is very challenging for a modern reader. One cannot consider Twain an anti-rascist. He is a racist.

The nature of strength and power is explored deeply by Hobb. Kennet's power is brittle. He seeks power and strength to protect himself from a terrible past and from the deep insecurity and fear he always fears. He was Igot's victim and he will never be a victim again - and so he creates victims and manipulates others. If he dominates and is feared then he is safe. Kennet does good only in the service of keeping himself in place. His power is used to selfish ends and by accident does good. The Satrap Cosgo has power through entitlement, he has earned nothing. He consumes those around him because he can and he has never understood his obligations to others.

Kennet is the most complex and interesting of characters in Hobb's universe so far. He has condemned himself to repeat the horrors of his past by using his abuser, Igot, as his model for power. He is aware at times that he is repeating a pattern. His rape of Althea a grim re-telling of his own rapes as a child. Hobb's description of Kennet's sexual preferences when using Eta as a prostitute are made clear. He does not know how to love only use. He was forced to be still and quiet when he was used and so he knows only one way to have sex. With Eta he pays her to be still and quiet. With Althea he drugs her.

Kennit's wrist charm is the embodiment of both his conscience and self-awareness. Its taunts reflect the pangs of his conscience and self awareness. Kennit's tragedy is summed up nicely by the charm:

> To defeat the monster, you became the monster ...You only have your self to fear now.

Kennit's charm is the evidence and symbol of self-awareness. He chooses to wear his accuser even when it brings him pain. This hints that redemption may be possible should he choose it. Kennit's end is ambiguous. He is embraced by Paragon and becomes part of a larger whole. Is Hobb making an argument for acceptance of all parts of ourselves - good and bad, broken and whole? She undoubtedly is. Paragon is broken, like a vase, until his other broken part (Kennit) is joined with him.

Then there is the power that comes from weakness, love and loyalty. Ronica and Keffria are reduced to penury, but they fight relentlessly not only for their own family but others. They remain loyal to Davad Restart even his memory when it is dangerous to do so. Their power comes from perseverance, moral clarity and love.

The dragon Tintaglia Power is immense. But it is like that of a child. It knows no hate but she is like a toddler pulling the wings off a fly. Her use of power is clumsy and terrible; she creates chaos. It is only through the wisdom of those directing her (Reyn, Malta, Selden) that she achieves her ends. Only by submitting (albeit unconsciously) to the wills of others does her power achieve her aims.

There is another type of power which is the strength that comes through self-awareness, reflection and growth. Malta and Wintrow embody this. Malta begins as an entitled teenager. Unable to empathise with her family or understand the risks she faces in the world. Where Malta is selfish Wintrow is self-less. Where Malta demands Wintrow quietly resists. Both find happiness and their place through accepting that the world is not as they thought it would be. Wintrow resists becoming a priest but realises, through heartbreak, sacrifice and pain that his vocation is to serve "Sa" in the world. Malta only begins to grow once she lets love rather than obligation guide her. It takes the shock of her father's death and her love for Reyn for her to finally wake up. And wake up she does. Hobb write's Malta as though she is in a dream and finally wakes up in the real world guiding the Satrap down the Rain River.

The liveships also have power, but power corralled, dominated and harnessed. They are imprisoned in bodies not of their own, dependent on others to act. They are a metaphor and a question. Does the love for our family empower or imprison us? Do we perform our role out of obligation, love or because we know better. The answer here is written in how the ships behave. Vivacia, Wintrow and Paragon, accept their what they are and choose what they value in life and grasp it. Others resist. The lesson here is that finding what is valuable and good and accepting and embracing it in oneself is a path to happiness.

This was the most enjoyable of the Liveship traders by far. Hobb brings the emotional weight and tension together in a beautiful crescendo. Her focus on the inner life and conflict of her characters is what brings weight and depth to the plot and narrative. She is able to inhabit the inner lives of monsters (Kennit, Cosgo) and heroes (Brashen, Malta) with both understanding and compassion. The threads of the _Realm of The Elderlings_ have started to pull together. We see the world and it starts to take shape. 

Much like Erikson's Malazan nothing much is explained. How are the Dragons of the Rain River and the Stone Dragons of the mountains related? Where is the "Skill" and the "Wit"? Where is the story going and too what purpose? Hobb gives us few or no clues to these questions. She leads us on into the unknown, her characters and their lives pulling us with them. Where Erikson plays with the readers frustration teasing them with unresolved threads and blind alleys Hobb's progression feels more linear, that we are building in a direction that is both intangible but more directed, like a river flowing to its end.
